## Basics

The spine has three sections: cervical (neck), thoracic (upper back, behind the shoulders and chest), and lumbar (low back). Like a lot of people who sit at a computer, almost all of my neck and shoulder problems traced back to a stiff thoracic spine, and they also hurt my swim technique. N=1, but within a week of working on thoracic mobility my swimming felt more streamlined and my neck was less stiff and painful at work. That's why this battery tests the T-spine along with the neck and shoulders.

General testing rules are in [[A2 - How to assess mobility]].

Neck targets use the AAOS normative ranges from [Physiopedia: Cervical Spine Range of Motion](https://www.physio-pedia.com/Cervical_Spine_Range_of_Motion): the low end of each range is the minimum, the high end is the ideal. Neck range drops with age, so treat the ideal as a young-adult number.

Each test is laid out the way the app defines it: ID · movement type · camera view · sides. IDs marked *(in app)* already exist in the app's protocol. Keep them unchanged, since past captures are stored under them.

## Core tests

Run all of these at every retest, in this order. They're grouped by body position so you only change position four times. Test both sides and start with the weaker one.

### Standing

#### Neck side bending
`neck-lateral-flexion` · Held position · Front view · Left & right
- **Setup:** Stand facing the camera, relaxed, with your head and shoulders in frame.
- **Instructions:** Tip your ear toward your shoulder as far as you can with light effort. Keep looking straight ahead, and don't turn your head or lift the shoulder. Hold.
- **Angle:** Between vertical and your head's midline (the line from the bridge of your nose to your chin).
- **Minimum / ideal:** 20° / 45°; sides within 10°
- **Why:** Picks up upper trap and scalene tightness, which often goes with desk work and forward head posture. It can be one-sided even when rotation looks normal.

#### Overhead reach (supinated grip)
`supinated-overhead-reach` *(in app)* · Held position · Side view · Left & right
- **Setup:** Stand or sit side-on to the camera, holding a small stick or PVC pipe in the tested hand.
- **Instructions:** Keep your palm facing backward and raise the arm overhead as far as you can. Stop as soon as the arm starts to turn outward. Don't flare your ribs or arch your back.
- **Angle:** Between torso and upper arm.
- **Minimum / ideal:** 170° / 180° (arm by your ear)
- **Why:** This is the Leahy test ([video](https://youtu.be/Pg4X26PbdS4?si=YbroQZENNDCxHUO_)). The underhand grip tensions the lats and the muscles under the shoulder blade, so it's stricter than a plain overhead reach.

#### Shoulder extension
`shoulder-extension` · Held position · Side view · Left & right
- **Setup:** Stand side-on to the camera with the tested arm closest to it. Keep your whole upper body in frame.
- **Instructions:** With a straight arm and thumb forward, raise the arm backward as far as you can. Don't lean your torso forward or shrug. Hold.
- **Angle:** Between torso and upper arm.
- **Minimum / ideal:** 45° / 60°
- **Why:** Reaching back behind you is needed for dips, the bottom of a push-up, and the finish of a freestyle stroke. This is the only plane the rest of the battery doesn't cover.

### Seated

#### Neck flexion
`neck-flexion` · Held position · Side view · Both
- **Setup:** Sit tall on a chair, side-on to the camera, with your back against the backrest. Keep your head and upper back in frame.
- **Instructions:** Keeping your mouth closed, bring your chin toward your chest as far as you can with light effort. Don't slump or let your upper back round. Hold.
- **Angle:** Between horizontal and the line from your ear hole to the base of your nose, measured from your neutral position (looking straight ahead = 0°).
- **Minimum / ideal:** 80° / 90°
- **Why:** Quick to capture, and it completes the neck picture alongside extension, side bending and rotation.

#### Neck extension
`neck-extension` · Held position · Side view · Both
- **Setup:** Same as neck flexion.
- **Instructions:** Look up toward the ceiling as far as you can with light effort. Keep your back against the chair and don't arch your low back. Hold.
- **Angle:** Same line as neck flexion, measured upward from neutral.
- **Minimum / ideal:** 50° / 70°
- **Why:** Looking up is what breathing to the side and sighting in freestyle ask for, and it's often limited with forward head posture.

### Kneeling

#### Thoracic rotation test (lumbar lock)
`thoracic-rotation` · Held position · Front view · Left & right
- **Video:** [demo](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QqZAIMLtlTc&t=1s)
- **Setup:** Kneel and sit your hips back onto your heels. Put one forearm on the floor in front of you and the other hand behind your low back. Put the camera at the head end at shoulder height.
- **Instructions:** Rotate the elbow of the hand behind your back up toward the ceiling as far as you can. Keep your hips on your heels. Hold.
- **Angle:** Between the line across your shoulders and the floor.
- **Minimum / ideal:** 40° / 50°+; sides within 10°
- **Why:** Sitting back on your heels locks the low back, so this isolates rotation in the upper back.
- **Arm position:** Always test with the hand behind the low back, never behind the head. Why:
  - Hand behind the low back takes the shoulder mostly out of the test, so the result reflects upper-back rotation.
  - Hand behind the head puts the shoulder into external rotation and abduction. That stretches the pec and lat, so a tight shoulder or chest can stop the movement before the T-spine does. The result mixes T-spine and shoulder range.
  - The published reliability studies for this test use the hand-behind-the-back version, so the targets above match it.
  - The behind-the-head version is fine as a training drill. Its numbers aren't comparable with this test, so don't mix them.

#### Neck rotation (lumbar lock)
`neck-rotation` · Held position · Front view (top of head to camera) · Left & right
- **Setup:** Same position as the thoracic rotation test, but with both forearms on the floor, shoulder-width apart, like a plank on your knees. Hips stay on your heels. Put the camera on the floor in front of you, level, pointing at the top of your head. Your shoulders should be in frame too.
- **Instructions:** Keep a long neck with your eyes on the floor between your hands, and don't let your chin poke forward. Turn your head as far as you can with light effort (~20–30%). Hold.
- **Angle:** Between the line from the center of the top of your head to the tip of your nose, and a line perpendicular to your shoulders. Measuring against the shoulder line cancels out any upper-back rotation.
- **Minimum / ideal:** 70° / 90°; sides within 10°
- **Why:** With the top of the head toward the camera, the head turns around the camera's line of sight, so the rotation shows up as a clock-hand angle in the photo. It also shares the thoracic rotation setup, so the two run back to back.
- **Notes:** I'm more restricted turning right. Camera height and whether the nose tip is visible from this angle still need a trial run. If it isn't, use a headband with a stripe as the marker.

### Lying on your back

#### External rotation (supine)
`shoulder-external-rotation-supine` *(in app)* · Held position · Front view · Left & right
- **Setup:** Lie on your back with knees bent. Bring one arm out to shoulder height and bend the elbow to 90°.
- **Instructions:** Let your forearm fall back toward the floor. Keep your upper arm at shoulder height, your shoulder down, and your low back flat. Hold.
- **Angle:** Between your forearm and vertical.
- **Minimum / ideal:** 90° (forearm on the floor) / beyond 90°, which this setup can't measure
- **Notes:** I couldn't do this at first. My left is better than my right.

#### Internal rotation (supine)
`shoulder-internal-rotation-supine` *(in app)* · Held position · Front view · Left & right
- **Setup:** Same as external rotation. Pin the front of the tested shoulder down with your other hand.
- **Instructions:** Let your forearm fall forward toward the floor. Stop as soon as the shoulder starts to lift. Hold.
- **Angle:** Between your forearm and vertical.
- **Minimum / ideal:** 45° / 60°+; IR + ER total within 10° of the other side
- **Why:** If you don't pin the shoulder, your shoulder blade tips forward and makes it look like you have more range than you do.
- **Notes:** My right is better than my left.

### Lying face down

#### Pec length (prone)
`prone-pec-length` *(in app)* · Held position · Side view · Both
- **Setup:** Lie face down with your forehead on a towel. Put the camera behind you at shoulder height.
- **Instructions:** Actively lift both arms into a shallow Y, with your hands roughly in line with your ears. Keep your chest on the floor. Hold.
- **Angle:** Between your arms and the floor.
- **Minimum / ideal:** 30° / 45°
- **Why:** This is the active version of the pec length test. The passive bench version is a follow-up.

## Follow-up tests

Run these only when a core test fails or stops improving (see the active-first rule in [[A2 - How to assess mobility]]).

To work out *why* a test is limited, use a troubleshoot session: [[T3 - Overhead Reach]]. See [[T0 - How to troubleshoot]].

Compare sides: more than 10° difference points to the upper neck. It replaces the partner-run cervical flexion-rotation test. |
| IR fails                           | **Cross-body reach**                                       | Lie on your back with your shoulder blade pinned. Pull your straight arm across your chest.                                                                    | Compare sides. **Pain = stop.** If any test reproduces sharp pain (not just stretch), don't push through it. That's a PT question.

## Other setups in the app

These are kept in the app's exercise library (so past captures stay addressable) but aren't part of the shoulder program:
- `shoulder-flexion`: wall overhead reach. It's easier than the supinated grip, so it tells you less.
- `shoulder-internal-rotation-wall`, `shoulder-internal-rotation-seated`, `shoulder-external-rotation-seated`: wall and half-sitting rotation. Supine with the shoulder pinned is the most isolated setup, so rotation is measured that way only.
