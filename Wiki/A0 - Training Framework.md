The system has four training layers. The first three are universal: everyone should meet the same minimum standards. Only the last one depends on what you want to accomplish.

| Layer                       | Question it answers                                               | Examples                                                                                  | Who it applies to |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------- |
| **1. Mobility**             | Can each joint reach its full range, and can you control it there? | Hip rotation, shoulder flexion, ankle dorsiflexion                                        | Everyone          |
| **2. Movement**             | Can you use that range in a coordinated, multi-joint pattern?     | Hip hinge, squat, single-leg step-down, push-up, foot and gait mechanics                  | Everyone          |
| **3. Foundational strength** | Can you produce force and tolerate load in the basic patterns?   | Pull-ups, push-ups, hinge, squat, carries, tendon capacity (e.g. single-leg calf raises) | Everyone          |
| **4. Sport-specific strength** | What does your sport or goal specifically demand?                 | Plyometrics, sport-specific loading, power, conditioning                                  | Depends on goals  |

Layers 1–2 take you from pain-free to moving properly (see [[A1 - Why am I making this?]]). Layers 3–4 build on that foundation.

## Mobility vs. movement

**Mobility** is joint-level. Can the joint get there, and can you control it at end range? **Movement** is pattern-level. Can you coordinate several joints to use that range under bodyweight?

That boundary tells you which bucket an exercise belongs in. A hip IR stretch with PAILs/RAILs is mobility. A single-leg Romanian deadlift that teaches the hip to hinge without the pelvis rotating is movement. Most PT exercises live in the movement layer.

## Building vs. maintaining a quality

If you were trying to work on mobility, movement, and strength at every part of the body all the time, you could easily be doing 2 hours of training per day. Luckily, you don't have to do every exercise multiple times per week forever. They key is that it's much easier to **maintain** a quality than to **build** it. For example, you might do 2-3 sessions per week to gain hip extension mobility, but need only 1 short session a week from then on to maintain it.

## Assessment is the loop, not a layer

Every layer has its own type of assessment, with its own tests and "good enough" standards. Each type is a separate protocol you can run on its own.

| Assessment type          | What it captures                                                                                   | Layer it informs                         | Status                                                                 |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Posture snapshots**    | Standing photos from the front, back, and both sides                                                | All (baseline)                           | [[F0 - Posture & Movement Screen]] |
| **Joint range of motion** | One active angle per joint, both sides                                                             | 1 – Mobility                             | [[U0 - Upper Body Mobility Assessment]], [[L0 - Lower Body Mobility Assessment]]         |
| **Movement screens**     | Squat, lunge, single-leg squat, step-down, hinge, held at the bottom position or captured at the extreme, from several angles | 2 – Movement                             | [[F0 - Posture & Movement Screen]] |
| **Strength benchmarks**  | Reps, hold times, and loads relative to bodyweight: pull-ups, hang time, calf raises             | 3 – Foundational strength                | Not started                                                            |
| **Sport analysis**       | Running gait video, swim stroke, sport metrics                                                      | 4 – Sport-specific strength              | Not started                                                            |

Posture snapshots are the odd one out. They have no pass/fail standard, and on their own they predict little. Their value is in spotting and tracking asymmetries, like a shifted pelvis, uneven shoulders, or one foot collapsing more than the other. Those point you to which range of motion and movement tests to prioritize. Posture and most movement screens are whole-body, so they won't split cleanly into upper and lower body pages.

The loop is the same at every layer: **test → train what fails → retest**. Once a test meets its standard, it moves to maintenance and your focus shifts to the next failing test.

## Layers are gates per body region, not a strict sequence

You don't need to pass every mobility test before you're allowed to deadlift. A failing lower layer **reprioritizes** the layers above it rather than blocking them:

- Keep training strength, but within your usable range.
- The region that's limiting you gets the most attention.
- For example, failing hip IR changes how you squat, but it doesn't stop you squatting.

Gating applies per region. A shoulder that fails the mobility battery says nothing about whether you're ready to load your hips.
