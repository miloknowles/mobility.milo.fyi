A running list of the research behind the methods in [[A3 - How to improve mobility]]. Each entry covers what was studied, what it found, and what it means for this system. Add new entries under the matching section, and keep the open questions list current.

**Evidence tags:** 🟢 meta-analysis or large RCT · 🟡 small RCT or systematic review with high heterogeneity · 🔴 no direct evidence, inferred or practitioner reasoning

Last updated: 2026-11-15

## Summary of where the protocol stands

| Step in A3 | Evidence | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Passive stretch (2 min) | 🟢 | Effect plateaus at ~4 min/session and ~10 min/week per muscle. 2 min × 5–7 days already reaches the plateau. |
| 2. PAILs/RAILs | 🔴 (🟢 via PNF) | No published PAILs/RAILs or FRC trial. PNF contract-relax is the closest proxy and works about as well as static stretching. |
| 3. Loaded eccentric at long length | 🟢 / 🟡 | Strength training improves ROM as much as stretching does. Eccentrics move peak torque to longer lengths. Fascicle-length gains are real but depend on the measurement method, and they reverse with detraining. |
| 4. Opposing muscle at end range | 🟡 | Small RCTs: active end-range work improves *active* ROM and retention more than passive stretching. |
| The full sequence | 🔴 | No trial of stretch → isometric → strength vs. any part alone. Adding stretching to strength training didn't increase *passive* ROM. |

## Static stretching

### Konrad et al. (2024). Chronic effects of stretching on range of motion: a meta-analysis. *Journal of Sport and Health Science*. 🟢
- **Design:** 77 studies, 186 effect sizes, healthy participants.
- **Finding:** Chronic stretching improved ROM, ES ≈ 1.0. Static (ES ≈ 1.0) and PNF (ES ≈ 1.28) beat ballistic/dynamic stretching. The difference between static and PNF was not significant. No clear relationship with total stretch duration. Significant gains start at ≥2 weeks. High heterogeneity (I² ≈ 75%).
- **For this system:** Supports Step 1 and backs PNF-style work in Step 2. Expect measurable change by week 2–4.
- Link: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2095254623000571

### Ingram et al. (2025). Acute and chronic stretching and ROM: dose-response meta-regression. *Sports Medicine*. 🟢
- **Design:** 189 studies, 6,654 adults (mean age ~27).
- **Finding:** Acute g = 0.63, chronic g = 0.96. No additional benefit beyond ~4 min per session or ~10 min per week per muscle. Intensity, sex, age, training status and frequency didn't moderate the effect. People with poor baseline flexibility gained more.
- **For this system:** Sets the dose for Step 1: 2 min/day is enough. Longer holds aren't worth the time.

### Ingram et al. (2025). Mechanisms of stretching-induced ROM change: meta-analysis. *Sports Medicine*. 🟢
- **Design:** 65 studies, 1,542 adults (71% male).
- **Finding:** Chronic stretching gave a small drop in stiffness (g = 0.37) and a moderate rise in stretch tolerance (max tolerable passive torque, g = 0.74). **No change in fascicle length** from static stretching, acute or chronic.
- **For this system:** Static stretching mostly changes tolerance, not muscle length. Only eccentric/long-length loading has evidence for structural change (see below).

### Thomas et al. (2018). The relation between stretching typology and stretching duration: the effects on range of motion. *International Journal of Sports Medicine*. 🟡
- **Design:** 23 articles.
- **Finding:** Weekly volume matters more than time per session. ≥5 min/week and ≥5 days/week was favored. Found static > PNF, which the larger Konrad 2024 analysis didn't replicate.
- **For this system:** Older and superseded on the PNF question, but agrees that frequency drives results.
- Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29506306/

### Konrad et al. (2025). Detraining effects after chronic stretching on ROM: meta-analysis. *Sports Medicine – Open*. 🟢
- **Design:** 13 studies, 556 participants. Detraining periods of 2–6 weeks after 5–15-week programs.
- **Finding:** ROM was still above baseline after detraining (ES = 0.55) but below its post-training level (ES = −0.41). Higher training frequency predicted better retention. Only physically active participants kept their gains. A high-volume, low-frequency program (2×/week) lost its gains within 5 weeks.
- **For this system:** For maintenance, keep ≥2–3 sessions/week. Frequency beats volume.
- Link: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-025-00935-5

### DePino et al. (2000). Duration of maintained hamstring flexibility after cessation of an acute static stretching protocol. *Journal of Athletic Training*. 🟡
- **Finding:** After 4×30 s of hamstring stretching, the gain lasted only ~3 min. Other acute studies report gains lasting 10–40+ min depending on dose and muscle.
- **For this system:** A single stretch opens a short window. This is the rationale for doing Steps 2–4 right after Step 1.

### Warneke et al. (2025). Practical recommendations on stretching exercise: a Delphi consensus statement of international research experts. 🟡 (expert consensus)
- **Design:** 20 experts.
- **Finding:** Recommends static or PNF stretching, 2–3 sets daily of 30–120 s per muscle. Stretching is not a primary hypertrophy strategy. Avoid static holds >60 s right before maximal strength or power work on that muscle.
- **For this system:** Step 1 fits within the consensus. Do the full protocol after training or in a separate session, not right before heavy lifting or sprinting.

### Warneke et al. — long-duration stretching and hypertrophy (calf orthosis studies). 🟡
- **Design:** Small samples of active adults. Calf stretch 1 h/day, 7 days/week, for 6 weeks.
- **Finding:** Strength gains up to ~22%, muscle thickness gains ~15%.
- **For this system:** Not relevant at a 2-min dose. Don't expect muscle growth from the stretch step.

## PNF / isometrics at end range (proxy for PAILs/RAILs)

### No direct PAILs/RAILs or FRC evidence. 🔴
- PubMed has no indexed paper on "functional range conditioning" or "angular isometric loading" (searched Nov 2026).
- **NCT03456050** (King Saud University): n = 56, chronic low back pain, FRC protocol with 2-min 90/90 holds plus PAILs/RAILs. Listed as completed in 2018, but no results were ever published. Link: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03456050
- ⚠️ A citation circulating online, "Spina et al., Int J Sports Phys Ther 2018;13(3):393–404" (a baseball RCT), matches no PubMed record. Treat it as fabricated. Don't cite it.

### Behm et al. (2023). Acute effects of stretching modalities on ROM: meta-analysis. 🟢
- **Design:** 47 studies.
- **Finding:** Static, PNF and dynamic stretching gave similar acute ROM gains (no significant difference).
- **For this system:** PAILs/RAILs is probably about as good as a stretch for passive range. Any extra benefit would come from building strength at end range.

### Sheard & Paine (2010). Optimal contraction intensity during proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation for maximal increase of range of motion. *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research*. 🟡
- **Finding:** Acute ROM gain peaked (13.3°) at about **64% of maximal voluntary contraction**. Other trials show submaximal contractions work as well as maximal ones.
- **For this system:** A3 currently says ~50% effort for PAILs. Consider 50–70%; there's no need to go to max.

### Contract-relax vs. antagonist contract-relax, calf (2022). *Frontiers in Physiology*. 🟡
- **Design:** Randomized, n = 40 healthy adults, 2 min of each technique.
- **Finding:** Similar dorsiflexion gains (+5.4° CR vs +6.1° antagonist CR). CR reduced muscle stiffness (shear modulus) more (−41.9% vs −12.5%).
- **For this system:** PAILs-style (contract the stretched muscle) and RAILs-style (contract the opposing muscle) give about the same range. They may work through somewhat different mechanisms, which is a reasonable argument for doing both.
- Link: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2022.1078301

### Sharman, Cresswell & Riek (2006). Proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation stretching: mechanisms and clinical implications. *Sports Medicine*. 🟡 (review)
- **Finding:** EMG data don't support the classic explanation (autogenic/reciprocal inhibition). PNF works mainly by raising stretch tolerance, with some viscoelastic stress relaxation.
- **For this system:** Avoid "override the stretch reflex" language. "The nervous system learns to tolerate and produce force in this position" is the better explanation.

## Strength training & eccentrics for ROM

### Afonso et al. (2021). Strength training versus stretching for improving range of motion: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Healthcare*. 🟢
- **Design:** 11 RCTs, 452 participants, mixed populations.
- **Finding:** No difference between strength training and stretching for ROM (ES = −0.22, p = 0.21), including in active vs. passive ROM subgroups.
- **For this system:** Step 3 earns its place: loaded work improves ROM by itself.
- Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33917036/

### Alizadeh et al. (2023). Resistance training induces improvements in range of motion: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Sports Medicine*. 🟢
- **Design:** 55 studies, 2,756 participants aged 10–70, 4–24 weeks, 40–110% 1RM.
- **Finding:** ES = 0.73 vs. controls. ES = 0.08 vs. stretching (no difference). **Resistance training + stretching vs. stretching alone: ES = −0.001.** Externally loaded training worked; bodyweight-only training did not significantly improve ROM.
- **For this system:** Use external load in Step 3. Don't claim the full stack beats stretching alone for *passive* ROM.

### Wiszomirska et al. (2026). *Journal of Clinical Medicine*. 🟡
- **Design:** RCT, n = 48 active young adults, once a week for 6 weeks.
- **Finding:** Both high-intensity stretching (2×60 s, +3.93 cm sit-and-reach) and full-ROM resistance training (+1.81 cm) beat controls.
- **For this system:** At very low frequency, stretching gave a larger raw gain.
- Link: https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0383/15/15/5783

### O'Sullivan, McAuliffe & DeBurca (2012). The effects of eccentric training on lower limb flexibility: a systematic review. *British Journal of Sports Medicine*. 🟡
- **Design:** 6 RCTs (hip, knee, ankle).
- **Finding:** Eccentric training consistently improved joint ROM or fascicle length.
- **For this system:** Direct support for the eccentric emphasis in Step 3.
- Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22522590/

### Brockett, Morgan & Proske (2001). Human hamstring muscles adapt to eccentric exercise by changing optimum length. *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*. 🟡
- **Finding:** Nordic hamstring curls moved the knee angle of peak hamstring torque toward longer muscle lengths within about a week.
- **For this system:** Eccentric training builds strength at long lengths, which is the main purpose of Step 3.

### Nordic hamstring training and biceps femoris fascicle length (meta-analyses; Yagiz et al. 2021, *PLoS One*). 🟡
- **Design:** 6–8 RCTs.
- **Finding:** Fascicle length increased (pooled g ≈ 1.09, I² = 79%). The result depends heavily on how fascicle length is measured: trigonometric equations g = 1.98, manual linear extrapolation g = 0.23, panoramic ultrasound g = 0.38 (not significant). Fascicles re-shorten after detraining.
- **For this system:** A3 says eccentrics "add sarcomeres in series." That's plausible, but the evidence is weaker than it sounds. Treat it as a hypothesis.

### Guex et al. (2016). Hamstring architectural and functional adaptations following long vs. short muscle length eccentric training. *Frontiers in Physiology*. 🟡
- **Finding:** Eccentrics done at long hamstring lengths increased fascicle length more than eccentrics done at short lengths.
- **For this system:** Supports the "pause in the stretched position" and "progress range before load" cues in Step 3.

### Lengthened-position training (Maeo et al. 2021, 2023; Pedrosa et al. 2022). 🟡
- **Finding:** Seated leg curls (hamstring lengthened) grew the hamstrings more than lying leg curls. Overhead triceps extensions grew the triceps more than pushdowns. Lengthened partials matched or beat full-ROM or shortened-position training for hypertrophy and strength at long lengths.
- **For this system:** When choosing Step 3 exercises, prefer the one that loads the target muscle at its longest position.

### Isometric joint-angle specificity (Oranchuk et al. 2019 review; Noorkõiv et al. 2014). 🟡
- **Finding:** Isometric strength gains are largest near the trained joint angle. Training at long lengths transfers across more angles and builds more muscle than training at short lengths.
- **For this system:** End-range isometrics (PAILs, Step 4 holds) mostly build strength near that angle. That's the point: strength exactly where the range is limited.

## Active vs. passive range

### Wyon, Smith & Koutedakis (2013). A comparison of strength and stretch interventions on active and passive ranges of movement in dancers. *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research*. 🟡
- **Design:** RCT, n = 35 trained dancers, 6 weeks.
- **Finding:** Passive ROM increased in all groups, with no difference between them. End-range hip-flexor strengthening and low-intensity stretching improved *active* ROM significantly more (20–30°) than moderate/high-intensity stretching.
- **For this system:** The best direct support for Step 4, and for the "active tests first" rule in [[A2 - How to assess mobility]].

### Meroni et al. (2010). Comparison of active stretching technique and static stretching technique on hamstring flexibility. *Clinical Journal of Sport Medicine*. 🟡
- **Design:** RCT, n = 65 healthy volunteers, 6 weeks. 22 reassessed 4 weeks after stopping.
- **Finding:** Active stretching gained 8.7° vs. 5.3° for passive (P = .006). After 4 weeks off, the active group kept 6.3° and the passive group kept 0.1° (P = .003).
- **For this system:** Active end-range work may make gains last much longer. Small sample, but a large effect.

## Open questions (no direct evidence yet)

- [ ] Does stretch → PAILs/RAILs → end-range strength beat any single part, for *active* ROM?
- [ ] What are the right PAILs/RAILs parameters? FRC uses 15 s ramps toward max effort. PNF research uses 3–10 s at ~60–70%.
- [ ] Do eccentric vs. isometric vs. concentric loading at true end range differ in how much active ROM they build?
- [ ] Is there long-term (>6 months) and trained-population data on retention?
- [ ] Do end-range strength/ROM gains reduce injury risk, beyond Nordic → hamstring strain data?

## Changes to A3 suggested by this evidence

- [ ] PAILs effort: ~50% → 50–70%.
- [ ] Reword the "adds sarcomeres in series" claim as a likely mechanism with mixed evidence.
- [ ] Note that Step 1 alone already reaches the dose plateau; the value of Steps 2–4 is active range and retention.
- [ ] Add a maintenance rule: ≥2–3 sessions/week.
- [ ] Timing: don't do the protocol right before maximal strength or power work on the same muscle.
